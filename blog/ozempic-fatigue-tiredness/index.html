Why Am I So Tired on Ozempic?

The short answer: Tiredness is a recognized side effect of semaglutide, the drug in Ozempic. In the Wegovy weight-loss trials, 11% of people on semaglutide 2.4 mg reported fatigue, compared with 5% on placebo. Most of the drain likely comes from eating far less, losing fluids to nausea or diarrhea, and, for some people with diabetes, low blood sugar. Fatigue that is severe, new, or lasting deserves a check for other causes such as anemia or thyroid problems.

Is fatigue a real Ozempic side effect?

Yes, but the numbers depend on which label you read. The Ozempic prescribing information (for type 2 diabetes) does not give a fatigue percentage in its main table. It lists fatigue, along with dysgeusia (taste changes) and dizziness, among "other adverse reactions with a frequency of >0.4%" linked to Ozempic.

The Wegovy prescribing information is more specific. Wegovy is the same molecule at a higher weekly dose for weight loss. In its pooled adult trials:

  • Wegovy 2.4 mg: fatigue in 11% of 2,116 people, versus 5% of 1,261 on placebo.
  • Wegovy 7.2 mg: fatigue in 11% of 1,311 people, versus 9% on 2.4 mg and 5% on placebo in that trial set.

In both Wegovy tables, "fatigue" also counts asthenia, the medical term for weakness. Ozempic doses top out lower than Wegovy doses, which may be one reason fatigue sits lower in the Ozempic label.

Can eating so much less make you tired?

This is the most likely driver for many people. Semaglutide works mainly by cutting how much you eat. In a 60-week randomized trial in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, people on semaglutide 2.4 mg ate about 270 to 290 fewer calories at a test lunch than people on placebo. That gap held at weeks 20, 40, and 60.

A 2026 scoping review in Obesity Reviews found energy intake fell 24% to 39% across studies of semaglutide and tirzepatide. The authors also noted that protein and micronutrient intake were rarely checked. Fewer calories means less fuel. Skipped meals and small, low-protein portions can leave you flat by mid-afternoon.

A narrower version of this problem is vitamin B12. Many people on Ozempic also take metformin, which can lower B12 over time. Our guide to Ozempic and B12 deficiency covers who should get tested.

Could nausea and dehydration be draining you?

Stomach side effects are common, especially while the dose is going up. In the Ozempic label's placebo-controlled trials, gastrointestinal reactions occurred in 32.7% of people on 0.5 mg and 36.4% on 1 mg, versus 15.3% on placebo. Most nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea happened during dose escalation.

Vomiting and diarrhea pull fluid out of the body. The Ozempic label warns that dehydration from these reactions has led to acute kidney injury in some people. MedlinePlus lists the signs of dehydration: thirst, dark urine, headache, muscle cramps, and, when severe, dizziness, confusion, and listlessness. Nausea also makes it hard to eat, which stacks on top of the calorie gap. Our Ozempic nausea tips can help you keep food and fluids down.

Is low blood sugar part of the picture?

On its own, Ozempic rarely causes low blood sugar. The risk rises when it is combined with insulin or a sulfonylurea (such as glipizide or glimepiride). The Ozempic label reports these rates:

  • With basal insulin: documented symptomatic hypoglycemia in 16.7% on 0.5 mg and 29.8% on 1 mg, versus 15.2% on placebo.
  • With a sulfonylurea: documented symptomatic hypoglycemia in 17.3% on 0.5 mg and 24.4% on 1 mg.
  • Alone (monotherapy): 1.6% on 0.5 mg and 3.8% on 1 mg, with no severe episodes.

The label's patient information lists drowsiness, weakness, confusion, shakiness, sweating, and hunger among the signs of low blood sugar. If your tiredness comes with any of these, check your glucose if you can. Tell your prescriber, since the dose of insulin or a sulfonylurea may need adjusting. Do not change those doses on your own.

Does losing muscle play a role?

Fast weight loss takes some muscle with it. The Obesity Reviews scoping review found that lean tissue made up as much as 40% of total weight lost in some studies. Less muscle can make stairs, workouts, and long days feel harder.

The weight loss itself is large. In the STEP 1 trial in the New England Journal of Medicine, adults on semaglutide 2.4 mg lost an average 14.9% of body weight over 68 weeks, versus 2.4% on placebo. The same trial also found a greater gain in self-reported physical functioning with semaglutide. So for many people, energy improves as weight comes off. Our article on Ozempic and muscle loss covers protein and strength training in more depth.

What can help you feel less tired?

MedlinePlus advice on fatigue lines up with what helps on a GLP-1 drug:

  1. Eat on a schedule. Small, regular meals with protein at each one help, even when you are not hungry.
  2. Drink steadily. Sip water through the day, and more on days with vomiting or diarrhea.
  3. Keep moving. Regular exercise, including resistance work, supports energy and muscle.
  4. Protect your sleep. Some people report trouble sleeping on Ozempic. Our post on Ozempic and sleep problems covers that separately.
  5. Go easy on alcohol and caffeine. MedlinePlus notes that stimulants are not an effective fix for fatigue and can make it worse when they wear off.

If calories feel hard to plan, calories.md has tools for tracking intake. Fatigue that clusters in the first days after each weekly shot, or after a dose increase, often eases as your body adjusts.

When should tiredness send you to a doctor?

Do not assume every tired day is the drug. MedlinePlus lists common medical causes of fatigue, including anemia, iron deficiency, an underactive or overactive thyroid, depression, sleep apnea, diabetes, and kidney disease. A simple blood panel can rule many of these in or out.

Call your prescriber promptly if you have:

  • Confusion, dizziness, blurred vision, or little or no urine output (MedlinePlus lists these as reasons to contact a provider right away).
  • Vomiting or diarrhea that does not stop, which the Ozempic label flags because of kidney risk.
  • Repeated low blood sugar readings, or shakiness and sweating with fatigue.
  • Fatigue with fever, night sweats, or feeling cold, constipated, and dry-skinned (possible thyroid signs).
  • Tiredness that keeps getting worse after the first few weeks at a stable dose.

The bottom line

Fatigue is a real but usually modest side effect of semaglutide. The Wegovy label puts it at 11% versus 5% on placebo, and the Ozempic label lists it among less common reactions. Eating too little, losing fluids, low blood sugar with insulin or sulfonylureas, and muscle loss explain most of it. If tiredness is severe or lasting, ask your doctor to check for anemia, thyroid disease, and other causes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Ozempic fatigue go away?

For many people it eases after the first weeks, or after each dose increase settles. Most stomach side effects in the Ozempic trials happened during dose escalation. If fatigue persists at a stable dose, talk to your prescriber about other causes.

Is it normal to feel tired the day after an Ozempic shot?

Some people notice more tiredness or nausea in the day or two after their weekly injection. The FDA labels do not report timing of fatigue by day. If it happens every week and affects work or driving, mention it to your prescriber.

Is Wegovy more likely to cause tiredness than Ozempic?

The Wegovy label reports fatigue in 11% of people on 2.4 mg versus 5% on placebo. The Ozempic label, which covers lower diabetes doses, only lists fatigue among reactions above 0.4%. The two labels come from different trials, so a direct comparison is rough.

Can I take caffeine or energy drinks to fight Ozempic tiredness?

MedlinePlus says stimulants such as caffeine are not an effective treatment for fatigue and can make it worse when they wear off. Fluids, regular meals with protein, and sleep are safer first steps. Energy drinks can also upset a stomach that is already sensitive.

What blood tests help explain fatigue on Ozempic?

Doctors often check a complete blood count for anemia, iron levels, thyroid function, kidney function, blood sugar, and vitamin B12. The right panel depends on your history and other medicines. Ask your provider which tests make sense for you.
