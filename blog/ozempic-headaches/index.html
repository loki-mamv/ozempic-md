Why Does Ozempic Give You Headaches?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Ozempic itself is only a modest headache trigger. In the large semaglutide weight-loss trials, 14% of people on the drug reported headache versus 10% on placebo. Most headaches on Ozempic come from side effects around the drug: fluid loss from nausea or vomiting, low blood sugar, and big changes in eating, sleep, and caffeine. These usually ease as your body adjusts to each dose.

Is headache actually a listed side effect of Ozempic?

It depends on which label you read, and the answer surprises many people. The Ozempic prescribing information is based on trials in people with type 2 diabetes. Its main side effect table lists only nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, stomach pain, and constipation as reactions in at least 5% of patients. Headache is not in that table.

Headache does appear on the Ozempic label in two other places:

  • In the postmarketing section, under "Neurologic," as a reaction reported after approval. The label notes that these voluntary reports cannot give a reliable rate or prove the drug caused them.
  • In the patient guide, as a sign of low blood sugar.

The MedlinePlus semaglutide injection guide also lists headache among the side effects to tell your doctor about if it is severe or does not go away.

How often do headaches happen on semaglutide?

The clearest numbers come from the Wegovy label. Wegovy is the same drug, semaglutide, at a higher weekly dose for weight loss. In adults with obesity or overweight on the 2.4 mg dose, the label reports:

  • Headache in 14% of 2,116 people on semaglutide.
  • Headache in 10% of 1,261 people on placebo.

So roughly 4 in 100 extra people had headaches that may be tied to the drug. Many of the rest would have had headaches anyway. In a separate trial of the 7.2 mg dose, headache rates were 9% on 7.2 mg, 8% on 2.4 mg, and 7% on placebo. In teens aged 12 and older, the rates were 17% on semaglutide and 16% on placebo, which is almost no difference.

The takeaway: headache is real but not common as a direct drug effect. If you get one, a nearby cause is often worth looking for. For a side-by-side look at the two brands, see Ozempic vs. Wegovy.

Why would Ozempic cause a headache?

No study has pinned down one single mechanism. The labels and NIH sources point to a few likely paths.

  1. Dehydration. Nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea are the most common Ozempic side effects. The Ozempic label warns that they can cause fluid loss, and it advises patients to drink fluids. MedlinePlus lists headache as a sign of mild to moderate dehydration, along with thirst, dark urine, and a dry mouth.
  2. Low blood sugar. The NIDDK lists headache as a symptom of hypoglycemia, along with shakiness, sweating, hunger, and a fast heartbeat.
  3. Eating and drinking much less. Semaglutide cuts appetite. Some people skip meals, drink less water, or cut back on coffee without planning to. MedlinePlus notes that caffeine withdrawal, lack of sleep, and alcohol can all trigger migraine.
  4. Adjustment to a new dose. In the STEP 1 trial of semaglutide 2.4 mg, nausea and diarrhea were typically mild to moderate, short-lived, and faded with time. Headaches tied to these side effects tend to follow the same pattern.

Does low blood sugar play a role?

For most people on Ozempic alone, the risk is low. In the label's 30-week monotherapy trial, documented symptomatic low blood sugar occurred in 1.6% of people on 0.5 mg and 3.8% on 1 mg, compared with 0% on placebo.

The picture changes when Ozempic is combined with insulin or a sulfonylurea. The Ozempic label reports:

  • With basal insulin: documented symptomatic low blood sugar in 16.7% on 0.5 mg and 29.8% on 1 mg, versus 15.2% on placebo.
  • With a sulfonylurea: 17.3% on 0.5 mg and 24.4% on 1 mg.

If your headache comes with shakiness, sweating, or hunger, check your blood sugar if you have a meter. The NIDDK treats a reading below 70 mg/dL as low. Its advice is to eat or drink 15 to 20 grams of glucose or carbohydrates, then recheck. The Ozempic label says a lower dose of insulin or sulfonylurea may be needed, which is a decision for your prescriber.

What does research say about GLP-1 drugs and migraine?

Here the evidence points the other way. Some early studies suggest GLP-1 drugs may reduce certain headaches, but the data are small.

  • A randomized trial in the journal Brain tested exenatide, another GLP-1 drug, in 16 women with idiopathic intracranial hypertension (raised pressure inside the skull). Exenatide lowered pressure by about 5.7 cmCSF at 2.5 hours and 6.4 at 24 hours.
  • An open-label pilot study in Headache gave liraglutide to 31 adults with obesity and hard-to-treat migraine. Monthly headache days fell from 19.8 to 10.7 over 12 weeks. The drop did not track with weight loss.

Neither study tested semaglutide, and the pilot had no placebo group. These results do not show that Ozempic treats migraine. They do suggest that a drug in this class is not a likely cause of long-term headache in most people.

How can you ease Ozempic headaches?

Most steps target the likely causes above.

  • Drink fluids through the day, especially after vomiting or diarrhea. The Ozempic label stresses this to lower the risk of dehydration.
  • Eat small, regular meals even when you are not hungry. Our Ozempic food plan has ideas that are easy on the stomach.
  • Get ahead of nausea. Less vomiting means less fluid loss. See our Ozempic nausea tips.
  • Keep caffeine steady. Cutting back suddenly can cause a withdrawal headache.
  • Protect your sleep. Poor sleep is a known migraine trigger.
  • Limit pain relievers. MedlinePlus warns that taking pain medicine more than 3 days a week can cause rebound headaches.

The label schedule starts Ozempic at 0.25 mg once weekly for 4 weeks before any increase. If headaches flare with each step up, tell your prescriber. For the full picture across GLP-1 drugs, glp1.md covers the whole class.

The bottom line

Headache is a modest side effect of semaglutide. In the Wegovy trials it affected 14% on the drug versus 10% on placebo. The Ozempic label lists it only in postmarketing reports and as a low blood sugar symptom. Fluid loss, low blood sugar, and changes in food, caffeine, and sleep explain most cases. Seek care right away for a sudden, explosive, or "worst ever" headache, or one with vision changes, confusion, weakness, fever, or a stiff neck. MedlinePlus lists these as warning signs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Ozempic headaches go away?

For most people, yes. In the STEP 1 trial, the main stomach side effects of semaglutide were usually mild to moderate and faded with time. Headaches tied to those side effects tend to ease as well. If a headache lasts for weeks or gets worse, talk to your prescriber.

When in the week do Ozempic headaches tend to show up?

No published trial has tracked headache timing by day after the shot. Many people notice side effects in the first few days after a dose or a dose increase, when nausea is also most likely. Keeping a simple headache diary can help you and your prescriber spot a pattern.

Can I take pain relievers for an Ozempic headache?

Over-the-counter pain relievers are commonly used for occasional headaches, but ask your doctor or pharmacist which one suits your health history. MedlinePlus warns that using pain medicine more than 3 days a week can lead to rebound headaches. Frequent headaches need a proper checkup, not daily pills.

Does a higher dose mean more headaches?

The data show only a small dose effect. In the Wegovy 7.2 mg trial, headache occurred in 9% on 7.2 mg, 8% on 2.4 mg, and 7% on placebo. Dose increases can bring more nausea, though, and fluid loss from nausea can add to headache risk.

When is a headache on Ozempic an emergency?

Get help right away for a sudden, explosive, or worst-ever headache. The same applies to a headache with vision changes, slurred speech, weakness, confusion, fever, or a stiff neck. If you also use insulin or a sulfonylurea and feel shaky or sweaty, check your blood sugar and treat a low reading.
